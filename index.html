Calculate your certificates Peak Demand Reduction Scheme (Amendment No. 2) Rule 2026
The short version

A battery on your site is now worth certificates

The Peak Demand Reduction Scheme (PDRS) is a NSW market-based scheme that pays for capacity to reduce electricity demand during the summer peak window of 2.30pm to 8.30pm. It does this by creating Peak Reduction Certificates (PRCs), which electricity retailers are legally obliged to buy and surrender each year.

Until now the scheme has been aimed almost entirely at households: batteries under 28 kWh, air conditioners, pool pumps. The Peak Demand Reduction Scheme (Amendment No. 2) Rule 2026 changes that. It creates three new battery activities and one dormant vehicle-to-grid activity, and it extends eligible battery sizes by three orders of magnitude.

For a commercial or industrial site, the practical effect is straightforward. A battery you were already considering for demand-charge reduction, solar self-consumption or price arbitrage now carries a one-off certificate payment that lands against the capital cost. On the NSW Government’s own worked cases, that payment is worth somewhere between a quarter and a third of the installed cost.

“Larger commercial and industrial batteries can deliver material peak demand reductions at scale.”

NSW Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water, Energy Security Safeguard position, July 2026.

The 2026 rule change at a glance

  • 1 July Amendment No. 2 Rule commenced. Definitions, air conditioning and the BESS1 and BESS2 changes are live now.
  • 1 Sept BESS3, BESS4 and BESS5 start. Implementation dates on or after this date only.
  • 20 kWh Entry point for the new business activities, measured as Usable Battery Capacity.
  • 30 MWh Upper eligibility limit under BESS5, with the incentive itself capped at the first 10 MWh.
  • 15 yrs Deemed lifetime for every install activity, paid as a single up-front certificate creation.
Mechanics

How the scheme actually works

The PDRS sits inside the NSW Energy Security Safeguard, established under Schedule 4A of the Electricity Supply Act 1995. Policy and the Rule come from the Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water. IPART is the Scheme Administrator and runs accreditation, registration and audit.

Certificates, not rebates

A PRC is a tradeable certificate representing 0.1 kW of peak demand reduction capacity, averaged over one hour. Certificates are created by an Accredited Certificate Provider (ACP), not by the site owner and not by the installer.

Liable electricity retailers must surrender a set number of PRCs each compliance year against their NSW load. That obligation is what gives certificates a market price, and it is why the value of an installation moves with the PRC spot market rather than being fixed by government.

In practice the ACP assigns the certificate value to the project, usually as a discount on the quoted price or a payment on commissioning. The site owner is the Capacity Holder and must nominate the ACP before certificates are created.

Equation 1: number of certificates Certificates = Peak Demand Reduction Capacity × Network Loss Factor × 10

Network Loss Factor is 1.04 in the Ausgrid network area and 1.05 in Endeavour and Essential. The factor of 10 converts kW to units of 0.1 kW.

Equation 2b: store and shift capacity Peak Demand Reduction Capacity = Peak Demand Shifting Capacity × 6 hours × Lifetime

Six hours is the Summer Peak Demand Reduction Duration, set by the peak window of 2.30pm to 8.30pm AEST. Lifetime is 15 years for BESS1, BESS3, BESS4 and BESS5.

Activity definitions

Six battery and EV activities, four of them new in 2026

Each activity has its own eligibility, equipment and implementation requirements, and its own equation for converting installed capacity into certificates. These are the battery-related activities as they stand under the amended Rule.

BESS1

Install a battery

Single dwelling and small business

  • 2 kWh to 28 kWh Usable Battery Capacity
  • Suspended for general installs since 1 July 2025
  • Reopened from 1 July 2026 only at Government Owned and Managed Sites, or through an Exempt Energy Program
  • Existing behind-the-meter solar required at the same NMI
BESS2

Onboard a battery with an aggregator

BCA Class 1 and small business

  • Existing battery joins a VPP or demand response aggregator
  • Eligible up to 50 kWh usable, incentive capped at 28 kWh
  • On-site solar requirement removed from 1 July 2026
  • Nomination window extended to 90 days from onboarding
  • Six-year lifetime, firmness factor 0.8
BESS3

Install a battery

New: apartments, from 1 Sep 2026

  • BCA Class 2 buildings with at least four dwellings
  • 20 kWh to 200 kWh usable, capped at 5 kWh per dwelling
  • Battery must be on the approved product list specified by IPART
  • Must be installed outdoors
  • Minimum customer payment of $1,000 excluding GST
  • 10-year warranty on each new item, 70% capacity retained
  • No existing battery at the same NMI
BESS4

Install a battery

New: small and medium business, from 1 Sep 2026

  • 20 kWh to 200 kWh usable, non-residential, not a data centre
  • Battery must be on the approved product list specified by IPART, currently the CEC approved battery list
  • Minimum customer payment of $5,000 excluding GST
  • Stackable with the Cheaper Home Batteries Program up to 100 kWh nominal
  • One BESS4 or BESS5 implementation per site, ever
BESS5

Install a battery

New: commercial and industrial, from 1 Sep 2026

  • 200 kWh to 30 MWh usable, incentive capped at 10 MWh
  • Battery must be tested to UL 9540A
  • No approved product list requirement and no minimum customer payment
  • Installed by a suitably licensed person
  • Development application and network approvals required
V2G1

Onboard an electric vehicle

Created but dormant

  • Bidirectional-capable EV with a battery of 40 kWh or more
  • Fixed demand response component of 1.3504 kW per vehicle
  • Six-year lifetime
  • Commences on a date the Minister nominates in the Gazette
  • Signals the market to resolve warranty and interoperability first

Both activities require the site to be connected to the electricity network in NSW, and the Implementation Date, the date installation of all equipment is complete, must be on or after 1 September 2026.

The two-tier incentive

Every business activity pays at one of two rates. The higher rate applies where the battery is installed within 90 days, before or after, of new solar PV capacity going in. The lower rate applies to everything else. Under BESS5 that is the difference between a 0.1 kW/kWh and a 0.067 kW/kWh demand shifting component, so roughly a third of the certificate value turns on the timing of the solar. Solar itself is not mandatory and the PDRS pays nothing for the panels directly.

Doing the numbers

From kilowatt-hours to certificates

Certificates are driven by a notional peak contribution rather than by measured performance. Three inputs decide the answer: the usable capacity, the inverter output, and whether new solar goes in within the 90-day window. The PDRS calculator runs all four steps below against your own figures.

Step 1: Battery Capacity

The Rule does not use nameplate capacity. Battery Capacity for the equations is the lesser of the Usable Battery Capacity, which since 1 July 2026 is defined as 90% of Nominal Battery Capacity, and four hours multiplied by the Battery Inverter Output. Under BESS5 a third limit of 10,000 kWh also applies.

The four-hour rule is the one that catches projects out. A 500 kWh battery behind a 90 kW inverter clears the six-times ratio and is perfectly eligible, but it counts as 360 kWh for certificate purposes, because that is all it can deliver across a four-hour benchmark discharge. Roughly a quarter of the certificate value disappears into an inverter that was sized for the load rather than for the scheme.

Step 2: Demand Shifting Component

BESS5.2: with new solar inside 90 days Battery Capacity × 0.1 kW/kWh
BESS5.3: all other cases Battery Capacity × 0.067 kW/kWh

BESS4 uses the same two rates but tiers them: the first 50 kWh earns at half rate, capacity between 50 kWh and 100 kWh earns at 70%, and systems above 100 kWh earn the full rate on the whole capacity. The tiering deliberately trims the incentive on smaller batteries that already do well out of the Cheaper Home Batteries Program.

Step 3: Peak Demand Shifting Capacity

Multiply the demand shifting component by the Firmness Factor from Table A6. For BESS1, BESS3, BESS4 and BESS5 the firmness factor is 1. Only BESS2 is discounted, at 0.8, because the capacity depends on an aggregator choosing to dispatch it.

Step 4: Certificates

Run the shifting capacity through Equation 2b for the deemed 15-year lifetime and six-hour peak window, then through Equation 1 with the network loss factor. Certificates are created once, up front, for the whole lifetime.

Worked example: 110 kWh battery with new solar

  1. 110 kWh nominal, so Usable Battery Capacity is 99 kWh. Battery inverter output 22.5 kW, so four hours gives 90 kWh. Battery Capacity = 90 kWh.
  2. Installed with 30 kW of new solar inside the window, so Equation BESS4.2B applies at the 50 kWh to 100 kWh tier: (50 × 0.1 × 0.5) + ((90 − 50) × 0.1 × 0.7) = 5.3 kW.
  3. Firmness factor 1, so Peak Demand Shifting Capacity = 5.3 kW.
  4. 5.3 × 6 hours × 15 years = 477 Peak Demand Reduction Capacity.
  5. 477 × 1.04 network loss factor × 10 = 4,961 PRCs.
4,961 PRCs Around $17,400 at a PRC price of $3.50. At 110 kWh nominal this battery sits above the Cheaper Home Batteries Program ceiling of 100 kWh, so the PDRS is the only battery incentive on the job.

Everything else that moved

The rest of the 1 July 2026 changes

The new business activities took the headlines, but the amendment reaches across the whole Rule. If you already deliver PDRS work, these are the changes that will touch your systems and evidence packs.

Usable Battery Capacity is now a fixed 90% of nominal

Clause 10.1 now defines Usable Battery Capacity as 90% of Nominal Battery Capacity, and adds Nominal Battery Capacity as a defined term taken from the approved product list. Previously usable capacity was read straight off the product list.

This is not a cosmetic change. It flows into equipment eligibility thresholds, into the inverter ratio test, and into every demand shifting and demand response equation. A battery that scraped over a capacity threshold on its old listed usable figure may not clear it on 90% of nominal, and vice versa. Equation BESS1.2 and Equation BESS2.2 were both updated to pick up the new definition.

BESS1 partially reopens, for government sites and exempt programs only

BESS1 has been closed to new implementations since 1 July 2025 under clause 6.10.1. The amendment adds clause 6.10.2, which lifts that block for implementations with an Implementation Date on or after 1 July 2026 that are delivered either at a Government Owned and Managed Site, or through an initiative that was an Exempt Energy Program at the Implementation Date.

Government Owned and Managed Site is newly defined as a site owned and directly controlled by the Australian Government, the NSW Government or a local council. Clause 8.1.1 was updated to add it to the eligible site list alongside Residential Buildings and Small Business Sites.

Other BESS1 changes: the warranty requirement for existing inverters is deleted, the minimum warranted energy throughput is now a single figure of 2.8 MWh per kWh of usable capacity, the DER Register evidence requirement is gone, and the activity is renamed to cover single dwelling and small business. Existing and new solar PV capacity must now be reported with every certificate creation.

BESS2 drops its solar requirement and lifts its size limit
  • The eligibility requirement for a behind-the-meter solar PV system at the site is removed entirely.
  • Maximum eligible battery raised to 50 kWh Usable Battery Capacity, though the incentive is still capped at 28 kWh in Equation BESS2.2.
  • The VPP nomination window is now within 90 days of onboarding and before certificate creation, rather than the previous tighter window.
  • Eligible sites narrowed to BCA Class 1 buildings and small business sites, replacing the looser reference to residential.
  • Warranty requirements relating to normal use conditions are removed. What remains is that participation must not void or diminish the equipment warranty.

An updated BESS2 fact sheet should be going to customers now.

Air conditioning: HVAC1 and HVAC2 realigned to GEMS

Both air conditioning activities were rebuilt around the Greenhouse and Energy Minimum Standards determinations. New definitions were added at clause 10 for Air Conditioner, Cooling Capacity, Ducted, Multi-split system and Single-split system, and the GEMS Registry definition was updated to match the GEMS Act.

  • HVAC1 now covers units up to 65 kW cooling capacity registered under the GEMS (Air Conditioners up to 65kW) Determination 2019, restricted to product classes 5 to 12 and 18 to 21. Low efficiency classes are out, multi-split is in.
  • A cap of 500 certificates per HVAC1 implementation now applies where one or more multi-split systems are used. System size is not restricted, but PRCs cannot be registered beyond the cap.
  • New equipment requirements 3 and 4 require model numbers to match the GEMS Registry, and for multi-split systems require a matching outdoor unit model number, a common manufacturer across indoor and outdoor equipment, and a manufacturer-approved combination.
  • New and replacement equipment must be designed and installed in accordance with AS/NZS 5141:2018.
  • HVAC2 is now limited to units of 30 kW cooling capacity or more, installed either at a Large Business Site, or as a replacement in a centralised system or common area of a Class 2 building. Large Business Site is a new defined term: a site entirely occupied by one business with an ABN that is a Large Customer.
  • Baseline AEER and minimum TCSPF tables were merged and reorganised by product class in both activities. Values may differ slightly from the tables they replace.
WH1 removed, and tighter equipment disposal evidence

Activity Definition WH1 has been deleted from the Rule along with every reference to it in clauses 6.9 and 11, Schedule A tables A4 and A6, and Schedule B. The activity was suspended on 19 December 2024. IPART will remove WH1 from an ACP’s accreditation at the next amendment, and will make contact directly where WH1 is the only activity on the accreditation notice.

Separately, clause 5.3(b) now states more clearly that replaced equipment must be lawfully disposed of, and that recycling evidence such as a tax invoice or recycling receipt must be obtained for any refrigerants disposed of.

Vehicle to grid is in the Rule, but switched off

Activity Definition V2G1, onboarding a battery electric vehicle with a demand response aggregator, has been written into clauses 9.1.5 to 9.1.8 and Schedule D. It commences on a date the Minister nominates by notice in the Gazette, and no date has been set.

The design mirrors BESS2. The vehicle must have a battery of at least 40 kWh and be enabled for bidirectional charging, the supply equipment must be on an approved product list, both must have at least six years of warranty remaining, and participation must not void or diminish any applicable manufacturer warranty for the vehicle battery or the charger. The demand response component is a flat 1.3504 kW with a six-year lifetime.

DCCEEW has been explicit about why: the activity is being published early to signal to manufacturers and aggregators that the market should resolve warranty, consumer protection and interoperability issues now, so the activity can be switched on later without a further rule change. Battery warranty was the single most common concern raised in consultation.

Transitional arrangements

New clause 11.12 draws a hard line at 1 July 2026. Implementations with an Implementation Date before that date must be calculated using the previous version of the Rule, the Peak Demand Reduction Scheme (Amendment No. 1) Rule 2026. Implementations on or after that date use Amendment No. 2.

Because the Implementation Date is the date installation of all end-user equipment is complete, a project that straddles the date is governed by when it finishes, not when it was quoted or ordered.

Stacking

What the PDRS combines with

The PDRS was designed to sit alongside the federal battery incentive rather than replace it, and DCCEEW added constants to the BESS4 equations specifically to avoid double-dipping producing near-free batteries at the small end.

Cheaper Home Batteries Program

Federal small-scale technology certificates are available on battery systems between 5 kWh and 100 kWh, and can be claimed alongside BESS3 and BESS4 PRCs. Two limits bite, and they are measured differently.

Eligibility is judged on nominal capacity. A system above 100 kWh nominal is outside the program altogether, not merely capped, so it earns no battery STCs at all. Above that line the PDRS is the only battery incentive, which is precisely the gap BESS4 and BESS5 were written to fill.

Certificates are then created only for the first 50 kWh of usable capacity, and since 1 May 2026 that allowance is tapered: the full STC factor applies to the first 14 kWh, 60% of it from 14 kWh to 28 kWh, and 15% from 28 kWh to 50 kWh. That taper is a large part of why NSW has stepped in for business-scale storage.

Solar small-scale technology certificates

Solar PV attracts its own STCs and is unaffected by the PDRS. Installing new solar within 90 days of the battery is what unlocks the higher PRC rate, so the two incentives point in the same direction on timing.

What does not stack

A site can only ever run one BESS4 or BESS5 implementation. Once certificates have been created under either activity, the site is closed to both permanently, so staging a battery in two tranches to claim twice does not work. For BESS3, the minimum customer payment is waived where the work is delivered through a Low-income Energy Program or an Exempt Energy Program, but non-cash inducements and in-kind payments never count towards a minimum payment.

Before you commit

Seven things to check on your site

  • Building class and use. Not a Residential Building, not a Data Centre. Apartment sites go to BESS3, not BESS4.
  • Prior implementations. Confirm no BESS4 or BESS5 has ever been run at the site, and for BESS3 that no battery exists at the NMI.
  • The four-hour test. Size the inverter against the battery early. Certificates are capped at four hours of inverter output regardless of how much storage you install.
  • The six-times ratio. Usable capacity must not exceed six times battery inverter output, which sets a floor on inverter sizing. Under BESS3 and BESS4 the figure that counts is the one recorded on the approved product list, so an off-list PCS paired with a listed battery has no number the test can be run against.
  • Solar timing. If new solar is in scope, land it within 90 days either side of the battery and at no less than a quarter of the usable battery capacity.
  • Approvals. Development application or complying development certificate, plus DNSP connection and commissioning requirements, before the Implementation Date.
  • Aggregator control. The battery has to be internet connectable and controllable by a Demand Response Aggregator, which is a procurement decision, not an afterthought.

The PDRS calculator tests the four-hour cap, the six-times ratio and the solar ratio for you, and flags where a system size quietly loses certificate value.

Who creates the certificates

Only an Accredited Certificate Provider accredited by IPART for the relevant activity can create PRCs. The site owner is the Capacity Holder and nominates the ACP. That nomination and the evidence pack behind it need to be settled before commissioning, because the Implementation Date is fixed by completion of the installation and there is no way to reconstruct evidence after the fact.

Evidence that has to be captured on site

For every BESS3, BESS4 and BESS5 implementation the ACP must report installer name and accreditation number, plus existing, new and total solar photovoltaic capacity, each broken into array capacity and inverter capacity. Where equipment is being replaced, lawful disposal and refrigerant recycling receipts are now explicit under clause 5.3(b). Build these into the commissioning pack rather than chasing them later.
