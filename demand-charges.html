    Mechanics

    One half-hour sets the charge

    A demand charge is not an energy charge. It prices the single worst half-hour, so the battery is not being paid to shift energy; it is being paid to make sure one reading never happens.

    How it is measured

    The meter records demand every 30 minutes. The bill takes the highest 30-minute reading inside the tariff’s demand window. Large tariffs bill kVA, which is real plus reactive power; small business tariffs bill kW.

    A battery only cuts the bill if it keeps that highest half-hour down. One missed half-hour sets the charge for the whole period, which is why control matters as much as capacity.

    Charge outside the window

    Charging inside a demand window adds to the measured peak and can cost more than the discharge saves. The battery has to be full before the window opens, and on Essential’s BLND3TO the charged window runs 7am to 10pm, which leaves overnight only.

    The three networks differ

    Endeavour Energy has the shortest window at 4 hours and resets every month. That is the most forgiving structure for a battery: a bad month costs one month.

    Ausgrid uses a 6-hour window, the longest of the three, and on EA302 and above sets the charge from the highest half-hour in the previous rolling 12 months. Lowering today’s peak does not lower the bill until the old peak ages out, and one bad half-hour keeps the charge high for another year.

    Essential Energy charges demand across most of the working day. Its shoulder rate sits within about 10% of its peak rate, so it is the only network where holding the whole working day down is worth chasing.

    Before you size anything

    What to check on a real site

    Which tariff is the site actually on?

    This decides everything else. Small business demand tariffs are optional on all three networks: EA256 on Ausgrid, N92 on Endeavour and BLND1AB on Essential. A small site sitting on a flat or energy-only tariff has no demand charge to reduce, so a battery saves nothing on this front, although the site may be able to move onto a demand tariff and then benefit. Endeavour’s default low voltage non-residential tariff is the energy-only N91, which carries no demand charge.

    Have you got the interval data?

    Request 12 months of 30-minute kVA data from the retailer. It tells you the real peak, how long the site sits near it, and how often it goes there. A peak that happens once a year needs a different battery from one that recurs every weekday, and without the data any saving figure is a guess. The site peak field on this page should come from that data, not from the connection size or the switchboard rating.

    Is the tariff billed in kVA or kW?

    Large tariffs bill kVA, which is real plus reactive power. Reactive power therefore counts against you, and the inverter’s ability to supply it becomes part of the saving rather than an afterthought. Small business demand tariffs bill kW. This page follows whichever basis the selected tariff uses and relabels the inputs to match.

    Can the control system actually hold the cap?

    The battery needs a discharge cap tied to a live demand reading, plus enough reserve to cover the whole window. On Ausgrid’s EA302 and larger tariffs the charge is set by the highest half-hour in the previous rolling 12 months, so a single miss is expensive: it holds the charge up for another 12 months and no amount of good behaviour afterwards brings it down early. Endeavour and Essential reset monthly, so a miss costs one month.

    How much energy does it really need?

    The window length sets the ceiling: six hours on Ausgrid, four on Endeavour, three for Essential’s peak alone and up to fifteen if the shoulder is covered too. Holding one kW off for a six-hour Ausgrid window needs six kWh at the ceiling. In practice sites rarely run above the cap for the whole window, so less energy is often enough, but that is a judgement you can only make with the interval data in front of you. The calculator above uses the ceiling case.

    What is not covered here?

    Stand-alone grid-connected storage tariffs have different structures and were not analysed: Ausgrid EA334, Endeavour N95, Essential BLND4SB and BLND4LS. Evoenergy in the ACT is not covered. Two source caveats are worth knowing: Ausgrid’s tariff statement says the window applies on “working weekdays” while a retailer summary says weekdays including public holidays, so confirm with the retailer; and Essential’s brochure states that a demand tariff applies once a site uses more than 160 MWh a year, but the brochure is undated, so confirm the threshold for 2026-27.

    Does this stack with the PDRS?

    Yes, and they are independent. The PDRS pays a one-off certificate value against the capital cost of the battery, while the demand charge saving is an ongoing reduction in the network component of the bill. A battery installed for demand-charge reduction is exactly the use case the NSW Government named when it opened the scheme to business batteries. Our PDRS calculator estimates the certificate side, and the scheme explainer covers eligibility.
